BMR vs TDEE: What Is the Difference? (With a Simple Example)

Last reviewed: August 2026 — By Ahmed Bream

BMR and TDEE are the two most misunderstood numbers in calorie tracking. Many apps mix them up, which is why people eat far below or above their actual needs. In short: BMR is the calories your body burns at complete rest; TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) adds everything else you do in a day. This guide explains the difference and shows why it matters.

BMR — the resting baseline

BMR keeps your heart beating, lungs breathing and brain working with no movement. For a typical man (80 kg, 180 cm, 30 years old) it is about 1780 kcal/day using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation. Read our full How to calculate BMR correctly guide for the formula and a worked example.

TDEE — BMR plus activity

TDEE = BMR + the energy burned by:

  • Daily activity — walking, standing, chores, fidgeting (NEAT).
  • Exercise — running, lifting, sports (EAT).
  • Thermic effect of food (TEF) — the energy used to digest what you eat, usually about 10% of calories.

A common approximation multiplies BMR by an activity factor: 1.2 (sedentary), 1.375 (light), 1.55 (moderate), 1.725 (very active), 1.9 (extremely active). Using 1.55, our 1780 kcal BMR example gives a TDEE of about 2759 kcal/day.

Why the difference matters

Your maintenance calories (the amount that keeps your weight stable) is close to TDEE, not BMR. Eating at BMR would usually create a large deficit. Most weight-loss plans aim for TDEE minus 300–500 kcal, never below BMR for long, because the brain and organs need that resting fuel.

FAQ

Is TDEE always bigger than BMR?

Yes, for every living person. TDEE includes BMR plus activity and digestion, so it is the larger number.

Should I eat at BMR or TDEE?

Your maintenance calories are around TDEE. Planning a modest deficit above BMR is generally safer than going far below BMR.

Do activity factors make TDEE accurate?

They are rough estimates and can overshoot or undershoot by 10% or more. Use them as a starting point and adjust from real-world results.
